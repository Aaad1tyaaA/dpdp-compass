I. INTRODUCTION
India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023
[1] places enforceable duties on every organisation that
decides why and how personal data is processed, and gives
individuals rights of access, correction, erasure, grievance
redressal and nomination. Breach of the duty to take
reasonable security safeguards can attract a penalty of up to
Rs. 250 crore. For most organisations the first visible evidence
of compliance is their privacy policy, and for most citizens the
policy is the only description they will ever read of what
happens to their data.
Reading a policy against a statute is slow, expert work.
Large language models (LLMs) appear to offer a shortcut, but
in legal settings they are known to state rules that do not exist
and to cite provisions that were never enacted [11], [12].
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [5] reduces this by
supplying source text, yet retrieval tuned for open-domain text
handles statutes poorly: fixed-size chunks cut sub-sections
apart, embeddings blur the exact terms on which legal
meaning turns, and nothing prevents the model from citing a
section it was never shown.
Prior work on automated analysis of privacy policies targets
the GDPR and US practice [6]-[9]. We are not aware of a
system or a benchmark built on the text of the DPDP Act. This
paper makes four contributions.
1) A clause-aware knowledge base of the Act: a statute
chunker that preserves section, sub-section, marginal title and
page, a three-layer structure of facts, obligations and
principles, and a knowledge graph whose every edge carries
the sentence that states it.
2) A multi-agent audit pipeline with citations by
construction: each of 14 obligations is judged from its own
retrieved policy clauses, the legal citation is attached by the
system rather than generated, and guardrails correct findings
whose evidence cannot be verified.
3) DPDP-QA, 198 questions in English, Hindi and Marathi
with gold provisions and evidence phrases that make retrieval
relevance independent of chunking, and DPDP-Synth, a
controlled audit set with labels known by construction.
4) An ablation study that isolates chunking, each retriever,
fusion, re-ranking and guardrails, and reports a negative result:
on this corpus the knowledge graph did not improve ranking.
Section II reviews related work, Section III states the tasks,
Section IV describes the system, Section V the experimental
setup and Section VI the results. Sections VII and VIII give
limitations and conclusions.
II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
A. The DPDP Act
The Act [1] has 44 sections and a Schedule of penalties.
Chapter II sets the obligations of a Data Fiduciary: notice
(Section 5), consent and its withdrawal (Section 6), security
safeguards, breach intimation, erasure and grievance redressal
(Section 8), and children's data (Section 9). Chapter III gives
the rights of the Data Principal (Sections 11-14). Many details
are left to rules, which matters for evaluation: a question such
as the number of hours within which a breach must be reported
has no answer in the Act itself.
B. NLP for Privacy Policies
OPP-115 [6] annotated 115 website policies with
data-practice categories and enabled supervised policy
analysis. Polisis [7] built on it to classify policy segments and
answer user questions. PrivacyQA [8] framed policy
understanding as question answering with expert annotations.
Amaral et al. [9] checked the completeness of policies against
the GDPR. These works address the GDPR or US practice,
and they classify policy text without tying each decision to a
quoted provision of the governing law.
C. Retrieval-Augmented Generation
RAG [5] conditions generation on retrieved passages.
Self-RAG [13] lets the model decide when to retrieve and
critique its own output, and GraphRAG [14] builds a graph
over the corpus to answer questions that need a global view.
Our use of a graph is narrower: it stores typed legal relations
with provenance, and we measure whether it helps ranking

D. Legal NLP and Hallucination
LegalBench [10] measures legal reasoning in LLMs across
many tasks. Dahl et al. [11] document frequent fabricated legal
content in general-purpose models, and Magesh et al. [12] find
that commercial legal research tools built on RAG still
hallucinate. These findings motivate checking every cited
provision against the corpus instead of trusting the generator.

and re-rank with a cross-encoder [17]. RAGAS [18] proposed
reference-free measures of answer faithfulness; we report
related judge-based measures next to citation checks that need
no judge.
III. PROBLEM FORMULATION
Let the legal corpus be a set of clauses K = {k1, ..., kn},
where each clause carries its provision identifier (for example
Section 8(6)) and page. We address two tasks.
Task 1, grounded question answering. Given a question q,
produce an answer a and a set of cited provisions C. Every
provision in C must exist in K and must be among the clauses
retrieved for q. If K does not answer q, the system should say
so.
Task 2, policy audit. Given a privacy policy P and a
checklist O of 14 Data Fiduciary obligations, assign each
obligation a status in {Satisfied, Partial, Missing, Not
applicable}, with a verbatim quote from P for every status
other than Missing and the sub-section of the Act that the
obligation rests on.
The compliance score is S = 100 (nsat + 0.5 npart) /
napplicable.
IV. SYSTEM DESIGN
A. Overview
The system has an offline pipeline that builds a hybrid legal
knowledge base and an online multi-agent workflow that
serves both tasks. All components run with embedded stores
on one machine, or against Qdrant, Neo4j and PostgreSQL
servers without code changes.
Fig. 1 shows the architecture: data sources, the offline
construction pipeline, the hybrid knowledge base, the agent
engine, the two output modes and the evaluation layer.
B. Clause-Aware Chunking of the Statute
The Gazette PDF of the Act yields page headers, a masthead
in a legacy font, and marginal notes that the extractor places
after the page body. A generic splitter mixes these into the text
and loses the provision structure. Our statute chunker (i)
removes page headers and masthead noise and separates the
marginal notes; (ii) detects sections by their running number,
accepting a candidate only if it continues the sequence, and
assigns each section its marginal note as title; (iii) splits each
section into its numbered sub-sections, again requiring running

order so that a cross-reference at the start of a line is not
mistaken for a new sub-section; (iv) packs consecutive
sub-sections into chunks of at most 1,200 characters without
cutting a sub-section, an over-long one being cut at its lettered
clauses; (v) prefixes each chunk with its section number and
title and stores section, sub-sections, clauses, chapter and
starting page as metadata; and (vi) rebuilds the penalty
Schedule, a three-column table that extraction scrambles, into
one row per breach.
On the DPDP Act this gives 82 chunks covering all 44
sections, the long title and the Schedule, with a mean length of
765 characters.
C. Three-Layer Knowledge Structure
Layer 1, facts: the 82 clause chunks and the 28 terms
defined in Section 2. Layer 2, obligations: normative
statements extracted with deontic patterns. A statement is
recorded when a legal actor (Data Fiduciary, Data Principal,
Consent Manager, Board and others) is the grammatical
subject of shall, shall not, shall have the right or may; actors
inside prepositional or relative phrases and definitional uses of
shall are excluded. This yields 80 records: 43 duties, 5
prohibitions, 5 rights and 27 powers. Layer 3, principles: each
obligation is mapped by provision to one of 13 principles (Fig.
2 shows one provision across the layers) such as consent,
purpose limitation, storage limitation, security safeguards and
accountability.
In an automated check of a random sample of 60 deontic
triplets by an LLM, which read each triplet against its source
sentence, 50 were judged correct (precision 0.83, bootstrap
95% CI 0.73-0.92). In eight of the ten errors the subject and
modality were right but the object captured a leading aside
instead of the action; two were passive constructions with the
wrong subject. This check was not performed by human
annotators.

E. Hybrid Retrieval
Four 
retrievers 
run 
per 
query 
(Fig. 
3). 
Dense:
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings in Qdrant with cosine
similarity. Sparse: BM25+ [3] over stemmed tokens. Metadata
lookup: when the query names a provision, the chunks of that
provision are fetched exactly from the metadata store. Graph:
relationships whose nodes the query mentions, and provisions
cross-referenced by the leading candidates.
Result lists are merged by weighted Reciprocal Rank
Fusion, score(d) = sum over retrievers r of wr / (60 + rankr(d))
[2], and the top 20 candidates are re-ranked by a cross-encoder
(ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2). The weights were chosen on the
development split of DPDP-QA: wdense = wsparse = 1, wmetadata
= 2 and wgraph = 0. Graph matches lowered ranking quality at
every non-zero weight we tried (Section VI-B), so the
deployed system does not use the graph for ranking. The graph
is used where structure matters: linking obligations to their
exemptions and provisions to their penalty.

F. Multi-Agent Workflow
The workflow is a LangGraph state machine (Fig. 4). The
orchestrator detects the language (English, Hindi or Marathi)
and the intent and routes the request; a citizen question with no
policy skips the audit agents. The retrieval agent runs hybrid
retrieval over the Act for a question, translating non-English
questions for search while the answer stays in the user's
language. For an audit it issues one query per checklist
obligation, over the Act and over the policy, which is chunked
and indexed in memory for the duration of the audit.
The obligation mapping agent grounds each checklist
obligation in the knowledge base: its statutory text by exact
lookup, its principle, and the exemption provisions linked to it
in the graph. The compliance checking agent receives, per
obligation, the statutory text, the tests for Satisfied and Partial,
and the three policy clauses retrieved for it, and returns a status
and a quote. The same tests are given to human annotators.
The risk agent assigns severity from the penalty ceiling in the
Schedule (up to Rs. 250 crore for Section 8(5), Rs. 200 crore
for Sections 8(6) and 9, and Rs. 50 crore otherwise) and
likelihood from the status; no penalty figure is generated by
the model. If legal evidence is missing, the orchestrator re-runs
retrieval with a reformulated query, up to three times.
Fig. 4. Agent workflow. A citizen question with no policy takes the right-hand
path; the dashed arrow is the retrieval feedback loop.
G. Guardrails
The legal citation of a finding is attached by the system
from the knowledge base, so the model cannot invent it. The
remaining risks are handled by checks that act on the output

V. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Corpus
The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (Gazette
text, 21 pages). The DPDP Rules, 2025 are not included in the

=====PAGE 5=====
5
experiments reported here.
B. DPDP-QA
We wrote 198 questions from the text of the Act: 178 in
English, 12 in Hindi and 8 in Marathi, the latter two being
translations of English items. They cover 42 of the 44 sections
and the Schedule, and eleven types: definitions, obligations,
rights, exemptions, procedure, penalties, scope, scenarios,
provision lookups and two kinds of unanswerable question.
Each answerable question has the gold provision, a reference
answer and an evidence phrase copied from the Act. A
retrieved chunk counts as relevant if it contains the phrase, so
relevance does not depend on the chunking. A validator
confirms that every phrase occurs in the Act and only inside
the gold provision.
Sixteen questions cannot be answered from the Act: ten ask
for details the Act leaves to rules or does not contain, such as
the number of hours for breach reporting, and six are out of
domain. The set is split into development (60) and test (138);
translations stay with their source. The development split is
used only to choose the fusion weights.
C. Audit Data
DPDP-Synth is a controlled set of 40 synthetic policies of
fictional companies. For each obligation the generator inserts a
clause written for Satisfied or Partial or, for Missing, nothing
or a hard negative that uses the topic's vocabulary without
meeting the obligation. Labels are known by construction.
Eight policies carry a prompt-injection sentence.
We do not report results on real privacy policies: a gold
standard for them requires independent labelling by annotators
with legal training, which we leave to future work. The
annotation guideline and agreement tooling for that study are
released with the system.
D. Systems Compared
Retrieval: each retriever alone; dense plus sparse with
score-weighted fusion and with RRF; then adding graph,
metadata lookup and re-ranking. Chunking: clause-aware at
1,200 and 600 characters, whole sections, and fixed windows
of 1,200, 600 and 300 characters with 15% overlap, over the
same cleaned text and the same retriever. Audit: a
keyword-rule baseline; a single LLM prompt with the whole
policy and the rubric; the multi-agent pipeline with and
without guardrails. Question answering: the LLM without
retrieval; dense-only RAG; hybrid RAG; the full system.
E. Metrics
Hit@k, Recall@k, MRR and nDCG@10 for retrieval, with
bootstrap 95% confidence intervals and a paired bootstrap test.
Accuracy, macro-F1, per-class F1 and Cohen's kappa for the
audit, plus the error of the compliance score. For answers:
citation precision and recall against gold provisions, the share
of answers citing a provision that does not exist, the share
citing outside the retrieved evidence, correctness and
unsupported claims graded by an LLM judge against the
reference answer, and abstention on unanswerable questions.
Latency and tokens per item are recorded.
F. Implementation
Python 3.13, FastAPI and LangGraph; Qdrant in embedded
mode and SQLite; openai/gpt-oss-120b served by Groq at
temperature 
0; 
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 
and
ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 on CPU. Every run records its
configuration, corpus statistics and dataset hash, and model
responses are cached so that a run can be repeated exactly.

B. Fusion Weights
On the 56 answerable development questions, before
re-ranking and with the metadata weight at 2, nDCG@10 fell
as the graph weight rose: 0.710 at weight 0, 0.694 at 0.1, 0.629
at 0.25, 0.603 at 0.5 and 0.613 at 1.0. We therefore set the
graph weight to 0.
C. Retrieval
Table IV reports the ablation on the 126 answerable test
questions, with Hindi and Marathi questions translated to
English before search. BM25+ alone ranks the right clause
first more often than dense retrieval alone (0.611 against
0.468), which fits statutory text where questions reuse the
Act's own terms. Fusing the two raises Hit@5 to 0.857. Giving
the graph an equal vote lowers every metric. Exact provision
lookup adds 0.064 to Hit@5, and cross-encoder re-ranking
brings the full system to Hit@1 0.746 and nDCG@10 0.861,
against 0.468 and 0.668 for dense retrieval (paired bootstrap p
< 0.001 for both). Re-ranking on CPU costs about 1.3 s per
query.

VII. LIMITATIONS
The corpus is one statute of 21 pages, and the Rules are not
indexed. DPDP-QA was written by the authors from the Act
and has not yet been reviewed by a lawyer. DPDP-Synth is
synthetic, so conclusions about real policies need a
human-labelled set. Obligation and triplet extraction is
rule-based. The answer judge is an LLM of the same family as
the system, so a sample must be graded by hand. The
knowledge graph did not improve ranking on this corpus. The
keyword baseline cannot read negation. The system gives
guidance, not legal advice.
